# Common Ground

## Helping Communities Listen, Learn, and Act Together

*Social Impact Brief | Resonance Technical Fellowship*

## 1. The Challenge: Communities Need Places to Think Together

Communities face problems that cannot be solved by individuals acting alone. Whether the concern is neighborhood development, education, local services, environmental stewardship, or the future of a community institution, progress requires people to exchange perspectives and work toward shared understanding.

Yet the opportunities for doing so are often limited.

Digital communication has made it remarkably easy to express an opinion but considerably harder to develop an understanding. Public conversations can reward certainty over curiosity, amplify disagreement, and discourage participation by people who are uncomfortable with confrontation.

Meanwhile, community organizers often lack simple, affordable tools for bringing people together, gathering their ideas, and turning those contributions into useful conversations.

The difficulty is not necessarily a shortage of good ideas or a lack of concern. It is often the absence of a practical process through which people can listen to one another, recognize common interests, and begin working together.

Common Ground begins with a simple premise: meaningful collaboration requires opportunities to be heard, a willingness to listen, and a process that respects both.

## 2. The Opportunity: Small Gatherings, Meaningful Conversations

Common Ground is designed around locally organized gatherings rather than large-scale public debates.

A community member, nonprofit organization, neighborhood association, or other local group can convene a gathering around a question that matters to its participants.

People can register to attend and contribute questions or ideas in advance. The organizer can use those contributions to prepare a more inclusive and productive conversation.

This approach offers several potential advantages:

- Lower barriers to participation. People can contribute an idea without needing to dominate a discussion or possess specialized knowledge.
- Better preparation. Organizers can see participants' concerns before a gathering begins.
- More balanced conversations. Contributions can inform the agenda rather than allowing the loudest voices to determine every topic.
- Greater local ownership. Communities define their own questions, interpret their own experiences, and determine what action, if any, should follow.
Common Ground is intentionally modest in scope. It does not attempt to resolve disagreement automatically or manufacture consensus. Its purpose is to help people participate in a process where mutual understanding becomes more possible.

## 3. The Solution: Practical Technology in Service of Human Judgment

A working Common Ground prototype has been developed and deployed.

Its foundation is a straightforward workflow: organizers create gatherings, participants register and share ideas, and organizers manage the information needed to prepare and facilitate the event.

The application also explores how locally operated, open-weight artificial intelligence can assist with the work surrounding community conversations.

AI could help organizers identify recurring themes, recognize connections among contributions, and prepare accessible summaries of participants' questions and ideas. These capabilities are opportunities for further refinement and evaluation, not claims of already demonstrated social impact.

The distinction is important.

AI should help people understand one another, not decide what they ought to think.

Common Ground's design direction therefore emphasizes human oversight, understandable results, and a clear separation between participant contributions and machine-generated interpretation.

The prototype uses a web application that can be reached through a hosted service, with AI capabilities supplied by a separately operated local model. This architecture allows exploration of alternatives to dependence on proprietary, centrally hosted AI services.

Local operation may offer meaningful benefits in cost control, adaptability, and data governance, provided the overall system is designed and operated with appropriate security and privacy protections.

The technology is a means, not the destination. Its value must ultimately be measured by what it enables people to do together.

## 4. The Potential Social Impact

The intended beneficiaries are people and organizations attempting to build more constructive forms of community participation.

Possible early adopters include neighborhood associations, churches, libraries, civic groups, and small nonprofits—organizations that regularly bring people together but may lack dedicated technical resources.

Success would not be measured primarily by registrations, page views, or the number of AI-generated summaries.

More meaningful questions include:

- Do participants feel their perspectives were heard and represented fairly?
- Do organizers find it easier to prepare inclusive, useful discussions?
- Does the process surface common concerns that might otherwise remain unnoticed?
- Are participants more willing to engage with perspectives different from their own?
- Do gatherings produce clearer next steps or opportunities for collaboration?
These are hypotheses to test through actual use, participant feedback, and iterative improvement.

A successful pilot would provide evidence about where Common Ground helps, where it falls short, and what must change before broader adoption.

## 5. The Next Step: Supporting Resonance's Work

Approximately fifteen hours of focused development have produced a functional application, a hosted deployment, local AI connectivity, automated tests, and repeatable demonstration data.

Common Ground was developed as a practical exploration of how accessible technology and locally operated, open-weight AI might support collaborative community engagement.

The prototype demonstrates an approach, not a predetermined fellowship project.

Resonance's work with interested and active participants provides the larger context in which such ideas may be explored, challenged, and refined. The opportunity is to contribute technical experience, curiosity, and a working example while learning from the priorities and experiences of the participants themselves.

My first responsibility as a fellow would be to understand and contribute to Resonance's objectives—not to assume that extending Common Ground should become the group's first task.

If the prototype proves relevant to those objectives, it offers a foundation for further experimentation. If the participants identify more pressing needs or better approaches, those should take precedence.

The underlying commitment remains the same: technology should serve the people and communities using it, rather than dictate the work they undertake.

Common Ground demonstrates what I can contribute, not a condition of my participation.
